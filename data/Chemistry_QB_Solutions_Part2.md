**1) (a) How many litres of oxygen at STP are required to burn completely 2.2 g of propane (C₃H₈)?**
**(b) Define: (i) Isobars (ii) Isotopes**

---

### Part (a): Volume of O₂ required to burn 2.2 g of C₃H₈

**Balanced equation for combustion of propane:**

$$C_3H_8 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 3CO_2 + 4H_2O$$

**Molar mass of C₃H₈** = (3 × 12) + (8 × 1) = 36 + 8 = **44 g/mol**

**Moles of C₃H₈:**
$$n = \frac{\text{mass}}{\text{molar mass}} = \frac{2.2}{44} = 0.05 \text{ mol}$$

**From stoichiometry:** 1 mol C₃H₈ requires 5 mol O₂

∴ Moles of O₂ required = 5 × 0.05 = **0.25 mol**

**Volume of O₂ at STP** (1 mole of gas = 22.4 L at STP):
$$V = 0.25 \times 22.4 = 5.6 \text{ L}$$

> **∴ 5.6 litres of O₂ at STP are required to completely burn 2.2 g of propane.**

### Part (b):

**(i) Isobars:**
**Isobars** are atoms of **different elements** (different atomic numbers, Z) that have the **same mass number (A)** but different numbers of protons and neutrons.

Example: ⁴⁰₁₈Ar and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca — both have A = 40 but Z = 18 and 20 respectively.

**(ii) Isotopes:**
**Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** (same atomic number, Z) that have **different mass numbers (A)** due to different numbers of neutrons in their nuclei.

Example: ¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C — both have Z = 6 but A = 12 and 14 respectively.

### Part (a): Oxidation Numbers

**(i) MnO₄²⁻ (Manganate ion) — find oxidation number of Mn:**

Let oxidation number of Mn = x

$$x + 4(-2) = -2$$
$$x - 8 = -2$$
$$x = +6$$

> **∴ Oxidation number of Mn in MnO₄²⁻ = +6**

**(ii) CO₃²⁻ (Carbonate ion) — find oxidation number of C:**

Let oxidation number of C = x

$$x + 3(-2) = -2$$
$$x - 6 = -2$$
$$x = +4$$

> **∴ Oxidation number of C in CO₃²⁻ = +4**

### Part (b): Lyophilic & Lyophobic Colloids

**Lyophilic Colloids ("solvent-loving"):**
These are colloidal systems in which the dispersed phase has a **strong affinity for the dispersion medium** (solvent). They are easily and directly prepared by simply mixing the substance with the solvent. They are **stable**, **reversible**, and the particles are heavily solvated (surrounded by solvent molecules).

Examples: Starch in water, gelatin in water, gum arabic, proteins in water.

**Lyophobic Colloids ("solvent-hating"):**
These are colloidal systems in which the dispersed phase has **little or no affinity** for the dispersion medium. They cannot be prepared by simply mixing. Special methods (e.g., Bredig's arc method, peptization) are required. They are **unstable**, **irreversible**, and easily coagulated by addition of electrolytes.

Examples: Gold sol (Au in water), ferric hydroxide sol [Fe(OH)₃], arsenic sulphide sol (As₂S₃).

| Property | Lyophilic | Lyophobic |
|---|---|---|
| Affinity for solvent | High | Low |
| Stability | High | Low |
| Reversibility | Reversible | Irreversible |
| Preparation | Easy (direct mixing) | Needs special methods |
| Effect of electrolytes | Less affected | Easily coagulated |

**3) (i) Explain application of colloids in medicines**
**(ii) Write properties of emulsion**

---

### Part (i): Application of Colloids in Medicines

Colloids play a very important role in medicine and pharmacy:

1. **Colloidal medicines:** Many medicines are administered as colloidal dispersions because colloidal particles are easily absorbed by body tissues. Example: Colloidal gold and silver are used as antiseptics. Argyrol (silver sol) is used as an eye antiseptic.

2. **Blood:** Blood is a colloidal system — proteins (albumin, globulin) are dispersed in plasma. Understanding blood as a colloid helps in treatment of bleeding disorders.

3. **Colloidal sulphur:** Used as a fungicide and in treatment of skin diseases.

4. **Castor oil emulsion:** Used as a purgative (laxative).

5. **Medicines as emulsions:** Many drugs are administered as oil-in-water emulsions to improve their absorption and palatability. Example: Cod liver oil emulsion (rich in vitamin A and D).

6. **Colloidal calcium and iodine:** Used to supply these elements in easily assimilable (absorbable) form to the body.

7. **Dialysis (artificial kidney):** Based on the principle that colloidal particles cannot pass through semipermeable membranes, while crystalloids (small ions) can. Used to remove urea and other waste from blood in kidney dialysis.

---

### Part (ii): Properties of Emulsion

An **emulsion** is a colloidal system consisting of **liquid dispersed in liquid**.

Properties of emulsions:

1. **Two-phase system:** Consists of an internal (dispersed) phase and an external (continuous/dispersion medium) phase.

2. **Two types:** 
   - Oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion: Oil droplets dispersed in water. Example: Milk, cold cream.
   - Water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion: Water droplets dispersed in oil. Example: Butter, cold cream (some types).

3. **Requires an emulsifier (emulsifying agent):** Emulsions are inherently unstable; an **emulsifier** (surfactant) is needed to stabilize them by reducing surface tension at the oil-water interface. Examples: soap, detergents, proteins, gum, lecithin.

4. **Tyndall effect:** Emulsions show the Tyndall effect when a beam of light is passed through them.

5. **Brownian movement:** Emulsion droplets show Brownian movement.

6. **Coagulation:** On adding electrolytes or on heating, the emulsion breaks down (demulsification/breaking).

7. **Dilution:** An O/W emulsion can be diluted with water; a W/O emulsion can be diluted with oil.

**4) (i) Name the type of adsorption in which Van der Waals forces are present.**
**(ii) Write expression for Kp and give the relation between Kp and Kc for:**
**aA(g) + bB(g) ⇌ cC(g) + dD(g)**

---

### Part (i): Type of Adsorption with Van der Waals Forces

The type of adsorption in which **Van der Waals forces** (weak intermolecular forces) are present between the adsorbate and the adsorbent is called **Physical Adsorption** (also called **Physisorption**).

Key features of physical adsorption:
- Forces involved: **Van der Waals forces** (weak)
- Reversible, low heat of adsorption (~20–40 kJ/mol)
- Decreases with increase in temperature
- Not specific (any gas can be adsorbed on any solid)
- Multi-layer adsorption possible
- Example: Adsorption of gases like N₂, O₂ on charcoal

---

### Part (ii): Expression for Kp and Relation with Kc

**Expression for Kp:**

For the reaction: aA(g) + bB(g) ⇌ cC(g) + dD(g)

$$K_p = \frac{(P_C)^c \cdot (P_D)^d}{(P_A)^a \cdot (P_B)^b}$$

Where P_A, P_B, P_C, P_D are the partial pressures of the respective gases at equilibrium.

**Relation between Kp and Kc:**

Using the ideal gas equation: P = CRT (where C = molar concentration = n/V)

$$K_p = K_c \cdot (RT)^{\Delta n_g}$$

Where:
- R = Universal gas constant = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹
- T = Temperature in Kelvin
- **Δnₘ = (c + d) − (a + b)** = change in moles of gaseous products minus gaseous reactants

**Cases:**
- If Δnₘ = 0: Kp = Kc
- If Δnₘ > 0: Kp > Kc
- If Δnₘ < 0: Kp < Kc

**5) (i) What is alpha (α) and beta (β) decay?**
**(ii) Identify the following reactions as α, β, or γ decay:**

---

### Part (i): Alpha and Beta Decay

**Alpha (α) Decay:**
Alpha decay is a type of radioactive decay in which an **alpha particle (⁴₂He — 2 protons + 2 neutrons)** is spontaneously emitted from an unstable nucleus.

- Mass number decreases by **4**, atomic number decreases by **2**.
- General equation:
$$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2} Y + ^4_2He$$
- Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He
- Alpha particles have low penetrating power but high ionizing power.

**Beta (β) Decay:**
Beta decay is of two types:

**β⁻ decay:** A **neutron** in the nucleus converts to a proton with emission of an **electron (β⁻ particle)** and an **antineutrino (ν̄ₑ)**.
- Mass number remains same; atomic number increases by **1**.
$$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^A_{Z+1} Y + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e$$
- Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻

**β⁺ decay:** A **proton** converts to a neutron with emission of a **positron (e⁺)** and a **neutrino (νₑ)**.
- Mass number remains same; atomic number decreases by **1**.
$$^A_Z X \rightarrow ^A_{Z-1} Y + e^+ + \nu_e$$

---

### Part (ii): Identify the decay type

| Reaction | Decay Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He + γ | **α decay** (+ γ) | ⁴₂He (alpha particle) emitted; A decreases by 4, Z by 2 |
| ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ | **β⁻ decay** | Electron (e⁻) emitted; A unchanged, Z increases by 1 |
| ²²²₈₆Rn → ²¹⁸₈₄Po + ⁴₂He + ν̄ₑ | **α decay** | ⁴₂He emitted; A decreases by 4, Z by 2 |
| ¹¹₆C → ¹¹₅B + e⁺ + νₑ | **β⁺ decay** | Positron (e⁺) emitted; A unchanged, Z decreases by 1 |

**1) Write a note on Markovnikov's rule with an example.**

**Answer:**

**Markovnikov's Rule** is an empirical rule that predicts the **orientation of addition** of unsymmetrical reagents (like HX, H₂O) across an **unsymmetrical alkene** (or alkyne).

**Statement:**
*"When an unsymmetrical reagent (HX) adds to an unsymmetrical alkene, the hydrogen atom of the reagent adds to that carbon of the double bond which already has the greater number of hydrogen atoms (more substituted hydrogen-bearing carbon), and the negative part (X) of the reagent adds to the other carbon (which has fewer hydrogens)."*

In modern terms: The **more stable carbocation** intermediate is formed during electrophilic addition, and the nucleophile (X⁻) attacks that carbon.

**Example — Addition of HBr to Propene:**

$$CH_3-CH=CH_2 + HBr \rightarrow CH_3-CHBr-CH_3$$

- C1 (CH₂=) has 2 H atoms; C2 (=CH–) has 1 H atom.
- By Markovnikov's rule, H adds to C1 (more H atoms) and Br adds to C2.
- Product: **2-Bromopropane** (major product)
- NOT 1-Bromopropane (minor/anti-Markovnikov product)

**Reason:** The secondary carbocation (CH₃–C⁺H–CH₃) formed at C2 is **more stable** than the primary carbocation (CH₃–CH=CH₂ → CH₃–CH–CH₂⁺), so the reaction proceeds via the more stable intermediate.

**2) What is the action of following reagents on CH₃Cl:**
**a) Zn, HCl   b) Na/dry ether   c) Mg/dry ether**

**Answer:**

Chloromethane (methyl chloride) = CH₃Cl

**a) Action of Zn, HCl (Zinc + Hydrochloric acid) — Reduction:**

$$2CH_3Cl + Zn \xrightarrow{HCl} CH_3-CH_3 + ZnCl_2$$

This is a **reduction reaction**. The alkyl halide is reduced to the corresponding alkane.
CH₃Cl → **Ethane (CH₃CH₃)**

*(Note: Zn/HCl reduces RX to RH in presence of H⁺; for coupling, Zn with another RX gives R-R)*

Actually the standard reaction for CH₃Cl with Zn/HCl is:

$$CH_3Cl + Zn + HCl \rightarrow CH_4 + ZnCl_2$$

→ **Methane** is produced (reduction of CH₃Cl to CH₄)

**b) Action of Na/dry ether — Wurtz Reaction:**

$$2CH_3Cl + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} CH_3-CH_3 + 2NaCl$$

This is the **Wurtz Reaction**. Two molecules of alkyl halide react with sodium metal in dry ether to give a higher alkane (coupling of two alkyl groups).

CH₃Cl + Na → **Ethane (C₂H₆)** + NaCl

**c) Action of Mg/dry ether — Formation of Grignard Reagent:**

$$CH_3Cl + Mg \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} CH_3MgCl$$

This forms **Methylmagnesium chloride**, which is a **Grignard Reagent**. The carbon-magnesium bond is highly polar (C⁻—Mg⁺), making the Grignard reagent a powerful nucleophile and base used in organic synthesis.

**3) Explain Anti-Markovnikov's rule with suitable examples.**

**Answer:**

**Anti-Markovnikov's Rule** (also called **Peroxide Effect** or **Kharasch Effect**):

**Statement:**
*"When addition of HBr to an unsymmetrical alkene is carried out in the presence of organic peroxides (or under free radical conditions), the hydrogen adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogen atoms (less substituted carbon), and bromine adds to the carbon with more hydrogen atoms — i.e., the addition is opposite to Markovnikov's rule."*

This is also called the **anti-Markovnikov addition** or **peroxide effect**.

**Important Note:** The peroxide effect is observed **only with HBr**, NOT with HCl or HI.

**Example — Addition of HBr to Propene in presence of peroxide:**

$$CH_3-CH=CH_2 + HBr \xrightarrow{\text{peroxide}} CH_3-CH_2-CH_2Br$$

- **Normal Markovnikov product:** 2-Bromopropane (CH₃CHBrCH₃)
- **Anti-Markovnikov product:** 1-Bromopropane (CH₃CH₂CH₂Br) ← formed in presence of peroxide

**Mechanism (Free Radical Addition):**
1. Peroxide (ROOR) generates free radicals under UV light or heat.
2. Br• (bromine radical) is generated.
3. Br• adds to the less substituted carbon (forms more stable secondary radical).
4. H• is then added to complete the product.

The reaction proceeds via the **more stable free radical intermediate** (secondary > primary), giving the anti-Markovnikov product.

**4) How will you carry out the following reactions:**
**a) Phenol to Benzene   b) Acetylene to Benzene   c) Benzene to Benzene Hexachloride**

**Answer:**

**a) Phenol → Benzene:**

Phenol (C₆H₅OH) is converted to benzene by **distillation with zinc dust (Zn dust)**:

$$C_6H_5OH + Zn \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} C_6H_6 + ZnO$$

The hydroxyl (–OH) group is removed (dehydroxylation), and benzene is obtained. This is a **reduction reaction** where Zn acts as a reducing agent.

**b) Acetylene (Ethyne) → Benzene:**

Three molecules of acetylene undergo **cyclic trimerisation** when passed through a red-hot iron tube at about 873 K (600°C):

$$3HC \equiv CH \xrightarrow{Fe \text{ tube, } 873K} C_6H_6$$

This is called the **Berthelot reaction** or trimerisation of acetylene. Three C≡C bonds come together to form the benzene ring.

**c) Benzene → Benzene Hexachloride (BHC):**

Benzene reacts with **chlorine (Cl₂) in the presence of UV light (sunlight)** — this is an **addition reaction** (NOT substitution, unlike in the dark):

$$C_6H_6 + 3Cl_2 \xrightarrow{\text{UV light / sunlight}} C_6H_6Cl_6$$

The product is **1,2,3,4,5,6-hexachlorocyclohexane**, commonly called **Benzene Hexachloride (BHC)** or **Gammexane/Lindane**. It is/was used as an insecticide.

**6) State octet rule. Explain its limitation with respect to:**
**a) Expanded octet   b) Incomplete octet**

**Answer:**

**Octet Rule:**
The **octet rule** states that *atoms of elements (especially main group elements) tend to combine in such a way that each atom acquires 8 electrons in its outermost (valence) shell*, thereby attaining the stable electronic configuration of a noble gas. This is achieved by sharing, gaining, or losing electrons.

Example: In NaCl, Na loses 1 electron (→ 8 electrons in outer shell) and Cl gains 1 electron (→ 8 electrons).

---

**Limitations of the Octet Rule:**

**a) Expanded Octet (More than 8 electrons):**
Some elements, especially those in the **3rd period and beyond**, can accommodate **more than 8 electrons** in their valence shell by using d-orbitals. This is called an expanded octet.

Examples:
- **PCl₅ (Phosphorus pentachloride):** P has 10 electrons around it (5 bond pairs). Configuration of P: [Ne] 3s² 3p³ **3d⁰** — the empty 3d orbitals accept extra electrons.
- **SF₆ (Sulphur hexafluoride):** S has 12 electrons (6 bond pairs) around it.
- **IF₅ (Iodine pentafluoride):** I has 10 electrons around it.

Elements of the 2nd period (C, N, O, F) **cannot** have expanded octets as they have no available d-orbitals.

**b) Incomplete Octet (Less than 8 electrons):**
Some elements are stable with **fewer than 8 electrons** in their valence shell.

Examples:
- **BF₃ (Boron trifluoride):** B has only **6 electrons** (3 bond pairs) around it. Boron (Group 13) has only 3 valence electrons and can only form 3 bonds, leaving the octet incomplete. BF₃ acts as a Lewis acid (electron-pair acceptor).
- **BeCl₂ (Beryllium chloride):** Be has only **4 electrons** (2 bond pairs) around it.
- **AlCl₃ (Aluminium chloride):** Al has 6 electrons around it.

These electron-deficient compounds are highly reactive and act as Lewis acids.

**7) Distinguish between aliphatic and aromatic compounds. Write two sources of aromatic compounds.**

**Answer:**

**Aliphatic vs Aromatic Compounds:**

| Property | Aliphatic Compounds | Aromatic Compounds |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Open-chain (straight/branched) or non-aromatic rings | Contain a benzene ring or fused benzene rings |
| General formula | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (alkanes), CₙH₂ₙ (alkenes), etc. | No single general formula |
| Combustion | Burn with clean, non-sooty blue/yellow flame | Burn with sooty, smoky yellow flame (high C content) |
| C:H ratio | Lower carbon, higher hydrogen ratio | High carbon, lower hydrogen ratio |

1. **Coal tar:** Coal tar, obtained as a by-product during the destructive distillation of coal, is the most important source of aromatic compounds. Benzene, toluene, xylene, naphthalene, anthracene, phenol, aniline etc. are all obtained from coal tar.

2. **Petroleum (crude oil):** Petroleum refining and the catalytic reforming of aliphatic hydrocarbons (naphtha fraction) yields aromatic compounds like benzene, toluene, and xylene (BTX) through the process of **aromatization/reforming**.

**10) Explain cis & trans isomerism in alkene. Explain Reforming reactions with suitable examples.**

**Answer:**

### Cis-Trans (Geometrical) Isomerism in Alkenes:

**Geometrical isomerism** (cis-trans isomerism) arises in alkenes due to the **restricted rotation** around the C=C double bond. For this type of isomerism to occur, **each carbon of the double bond must have two different groups/atoms** attached to it.

**Cis isomer:** The two identical (or similar priority) groups are on the **same side** of the double bond.

**Trans isomer:** The two identical (or similar priority) groups are on **opposite sides** of the double bond.

**Example — But-2-ene (CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃):**

```
Cis-but-2-ene:              Trans-but-2-ene:
   CH₃    CH₃                  CH₃    H
     \   /                       \   /
      C=C                         C=C
     /   \                       /   \
    H     H                     H    CH₃
  (same side)                (opposite sides)
```

- Cis isomer: Higher boiling point (more polar)
- Trans isomer: More stable (lower energy; bulky groups far apart)

**Condition for cis-trans isomerism:**
- Restricted rotation (C=C must be present)
- Each doubly bonded carbon must have **two different substituents**
- Example: CH₂=CH₂ (ethene) does NOT show this isomerism (both H on each C are same)

---

### Reforming Reactions:

**Reforming** is a process in which **aliphatic hydrocarbons (from petroleum/naphtha fraction)** are converted into **aromatic hydrocarbons** by the application of heat, pressure, and catalysts. This process increases the octane number of petrol.

**Types of Reforming:**

**1) Thermal Reforming:** Heating aliphatic compounds at high temperature and pressure to form aromatics (without catalyst).

**2) Catalytic Reforming:** Using catalysts (Pt, Re on Al₂O₃ support) to convert aliphatic to aromatic hydrocarbons at lower temperatures.

**Examples:**

**Cyclisation (Dehydrocyclisation):** Hexane (straight chain) → Benzene + H₂

$$CH_3(CH_2)_4CH_3 \xrightarrow{Pt, \Delta, \text{high P}} C_6H_6 + 4H_2$$
*n-Hexane → Benzene*

$$CH_3(CH_2)_5CH_3 \xrightarrow{Pt, \Delta} C_6H_5CH_3 + 4H_2$$
*n-Heptane → Toluene*

Reforming is industrially important for producing aromatic compounds (BTX — Benzene, Toluene, Xylene) from petroleum.

**11) What is the action of following on Ethyl Chloride:**
**a) Zn, HCl   b) Na/Dry ether   c) Mg/Dry ether**

**Answer:**

Ethyl chloride = C₂H₅Cl = CH₃CH₂Cl

**a) Zn, HCl — Reduction:**

$$C_2H_5Cl + Zn + HCl \rightarrow C_2H_6 + ZnCl_2$$

Ethyl chloride is **reduced** to **ethane** by zinc and hydrochloric acid. The C–Cl bond is replaced by C–H.

**b) Na/Dry ether — Wurtz Reaction:**

$$2C_2H_5Cl + 2Na \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} C_2H_5-C_2H_5 + 2NaCl$$

$$\rightarrow CH_3CH_2CH_2CH_3 \text{ (Butane)}$$

Two molecules of ethyl chloride react with sodium in dry ether to give **n-Butane (C₄H₁₀)**. This is the **Wurtz reaction** — used to prepare higher alkanes from alkyl halides.

**c) Mg/Dry ether — Grignard Reagent:**

$$C_2H_5Cl + Mg \xrightarrow{\text{dry ether}} C_2H_5MgCl$$

**Ethylmagnesium chloride** is formed — this is a **Grignard reagent**. It is highly reactive (the C–Mg bond is polarized C⁻–Mg⁺) and is used extensively in organic synthesis to form C–C bonds, prepare alcohols, carboxylic acids, etc.

**12) How is the following conversion carried out:**
**a) Benzene → Chlorobenzene   b) Benzene → Nitrobenzene   c) Acetylene → Benzene**

**Answer:**

**a) Benzene → Chlorobenzene (Halogenation):**

Benzene reacts with **chlorine (Cl₂)** in the presence of a **Lewis acid catalyst (anhydrous FeCl₃ or AlCl₃)** — this is **electrophilic aromatic substitution**:

$$C_6H_6 + Cl_2 \xrightarrow{FeCl_3 \text{ (anhydrous)}} C_6H_5Cl + HCl$$

*Benzene + Chlorine → Chlorobenzene + HCl*

Mechanism: FeCl₃ activates Cl₂ to generate Cl⁺ electrophile → attacks benzene ring → substitution.

**b) Benzene → Nitrobenzene (Nitration):**

Benzene reacts with a **nitrating mixture** (conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄) at about **55°C**:

$$C_6H_6 + HNO_3 \xrightarrow{\text{conc. }H_2SO_4, 55°C} C_6H_5NO_2 + H_2O$$

*Benzene + Nitric acid → Nitrobenzene + Water*

The electrophile is the **nitronium ion (NO₂⁺)** generated by the reaction of HNO₃ with H₂SO₄. This is electrophilic aromatic substitution.

**c) Acetylene → Benzene (Cyclic Trimerisation):**

Three molecules of acetylene (ethyne) undergo **trimerisation** when passed through a red-hot iron tube at **873 K (600°C)**:

$$3HC \equiv CH \xrightarrow{\text{Fe tube, 873K}} C_6H_6$$

*3 Acetylene → Benzene*

This is the **Berthelot synthesis** of benzene from acetylene.

**1) What is the action of:**
**a) Zn on aq. NaOH   b) Mg on Dil. HCl**

---

### a) Action of Zn on aqueous NaOH (Zinc + Sodium Hydroxide solution):

Zinc reacts with hot concentrated aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to form **sodium zincate** and **hydrogen gas**:

$$Zn + 2NaOH \xrightarrow{\text{hot, conc.}} Na_2ZnO_2 + H_2 \uparrow$$

*Zinc + Sodium hydroxide → Sodium zincate + Hydrogen gas*

**Explanation:**
- Zinc is an **amphoteric metal** — it reacts with both acids and bases.
- With NaOH (a base), Zn acts as an acid.
- Sodium zincate (Na₂ZnO₂) is the salt formed.
- Hydrogen gas is evolved (can be tested with a burning splint — it burns with a "pop" sound).

This is also written as:
$$Zn + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2[Zn(OH)_4] \xrightarrow{\Delta} Na_2ZnO_2 + 2H_2O$$

---

### b) Action of Mg on Dilute HCl (Magnesium + Dilute Hydrochloric acid):

Magnesium reacts vigorously with dilute hydrochloric acid to form **magnesium chloride** and **hydrogen gas**:

$$Mg + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + H_2 \uparrow$$

*Magnesium + Hydrochloric acid → Magnesium chloride + Hydrogen gas*

**Explanation:**
- Magnesium is an **active metal** (alkali earth metal, Group 2) and reacts readily with dilute acids.
- It displaces hydrogen from the acid (Mg is above H in the activity series).
- Magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) is soluble in water.
- Hydrogen gas (H₂) is evolved with effervescence.

The reaction is a **single displacement (redox) reaction**:
- Mg is oxidized: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻
- H⁺ is reduced: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

**2) Explain the following:**
**a) Inductive effect   b) Electromeric effect**

---

### a) Inductive Effect:

The **Inductive effect** is the **permanent displacement of electrons** along a chain of carbon atoms through sigma (σ) bonds due to the presence of an electronegative atom or group. It is transmitted through the chain but **decreases with distance** from the electronegative atom.

**Types:**

**−I effect (Electron-withdrawing inductive effect):**
Electronegative atoms/groups (like –Cl, –Br, –OH, –NO₂, –CN) withdraw electron density through sigma bonds towards themselves, leaving the adjacent carbon electron-deficient.

```
δ+    δ+    δ+
CH₃ → CH₂ → CH₂ → Cl
(electrons withdrawn towards Cl)
```

**+I effect (Electron-donating inductive effect):**
Alkyl groups (like –CH₃, –C₂H₅) push electron density away from themselves towards adjacent carbons, making them slightly electron-rich.

```
CH₃ ← CH₂ → ...
(electrons pushed towards chain)
```

**Applications:**
- Explains acidity of carboxylic acids (–COOH) and their strength.
- Explains basicity of amines.
- Stabilization of carbocations by alkyl groups (+I groups).
- Inductive effect decreases rapidly with distance (negligible after 3 bonds).

---

### b) Electromeric Effect:

The **Electromeric effect** is a **temporary, reversible displacement of π (pi) electrons** of a multiple bond (C=C, C=O, C≡C) completely to one atom when the molecule is approached by an attacking reagent (electrophile or nucleophile).

It is an **instantaneous effect**, occurring only when a reagent approaches, and disappears once the reagent is removed.

**Types:**

**+E effect (Electron release towards attacking reagent):**
The π electrons are transferred to the carbon adjacent to the reagent's attack.

Example: In C=O, when a nucleophile approaches:
```
     :Nu
     |
   C=O  →  C⁻ — O (π electrons shift)
```

**−E effect (Electron release away from attacking reagent):**
The π electrons are transferred away from the point of attack.

**Example — Electromeric effect in ethylene with H⁺:**

When H⁺ (electrophile) approaches CH₂=CH₂, the π electrons shift completely towards one carbon:

```
H⁺ + CH₂=CH₂  →  CH₂⁺ — CH₃
                  (carbocation formed)
```

**Key features:**
- Temporary effect (only in presence of reagent)
- Involves complete transfer of π electrons
- Only affects π bonds (double/triple bonds)
- It is distinct from inductive effect (which is permanent and involves σ bonds)

**3) a) What is a homologous series? Write 1st two members of homologous series of alkyne.**
**b) Define Analgesics   c) Define Antioxidants**

### a) Homologous Series & First Two Members of Alkyne:

**Homologous Series:**
A **homologous series** is a group of organic compounds that:
1. Have the **same general formula** and same functional group
2. Differ from each other by a **–CH₂– (methylene) unit** in their molecular formula
3. Show a **gradual and regular change in physical properties** (boiling point, melting point, density) with increasing molecular mass
4. Have **similar chemical properties** due to the same functional group

Example: Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂): CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈...

**First two members of the homologous series of Alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂):**

1. **Ethyne (Acetylene):** CH≡CH — C₂H₂
2. **Propyne:** CH₃–C≡CH — C₃H₄

---

### b) Analgesics:

**Analgesics** are drugs (medicines) that **relieve pain** without causing loss of consciousness. They are also called **pain killers**.

They are of two types:
- **Non-narcotic analgesics:** Mild, non-habit-forming. E.g., Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid), Paracetamol, Ibuprofen — used for mild to moderate pain like headache, toothache.
- **Narcotic analgesics:** Strong, habit-forming (addictive). E.g., Morphine, Codeine — used for severe pain (post-surgery, cancer pain) under medical supervision.

---

### c) Antioxidants:

**Antioxidants** are substances that **prevent or delay oxidation** of other substances (especially food and biological molecules). They act by **neutralizing free radicals** or inhibiting oxidative reactions.

**In food:** Antioxidants prevent rancidity (spoilage of fats and oils) and preserve freshness.
Examples: BHA (Butylated Hydroxyanisole), BHT (Butylated Hydroxytoluene), Vitamin C (ascorbic acid), Vitamin E (tocopherol).

**In biology:** Antioxidants protect cells from oxidative damage that can lead to cancer and ageing.
Examples: Vitamin C, Vitamin E, beta-carotene, selenium.

**4) Define Antibiotics. Write any two uses of alkanes.**

---

### Antibiotics:

**Antibiotics** are chemical substances produced by **microorganisms** (bacteria, fungi, moulds) that **kill or inhibit the growth of other microorganisms** (especially bacteria), thus curing infectious diseases.

The term was coined by Selman Waksman. The first antibiotic discovered was **Penicillin** by Alexander Fleming (1928).

**Examples:** Penicillin (from *Penicillium notatum*), Streptomycin, Tetracycline, Erythromycin, Amoxicillin.

**Types:**
- **Bactericidal:** Kill bacteria directly (e.g., Penicillin, Streptomycin)
- **Bacteriostatic:** Inhibit the growth/multiplication of bacteria (e.g., Erythromycin, Tetracycline)

**Important:** Antibiotics are effective only against **bacterial infections**, NOT viral infections (like cold, flu, COVID-19).

---

### Two Uses of Alkanes:

1. **Fuel:** Alkanes are excellent fuels. Methane (CH₄) is the main component of **natural gas (LPG)**; propane and butane are used in **LPG cylinders** for cooking; octane is a major component of **petrol/gasoline**; kerosene (C₁₀–C₁₆ alkanes) is used as **aircraft fuel**.

2. **Lubricants and waxes:** Higher alkanes (long chain) are used as **lubricating oils** in machines and as **petroleum jelly (Vaseline)** for skin care. Paraffin wax (C₂₀–C₃₅ alkanes) is used in **candles, polishes, and cosmetics**.

*(Additional uses: Solvents — petrol ether; petrochemical feedstock for making plastics, drugs, dyes)*

**5) How will you convert:**
**1) Ethyl Chloride to Ethene**
**2) 1,2-Dibromoethane to Ethyne**

---

### 1) Ethyl Chloride (C₂H₅Cl) → Ethene (C₂H₄):

**Method: Dehydrohalogenation (Elimination reaction)**

Ethyl chloride is treated with **alcoholic KOH (potassium hydroxide in ethanol)** at high temperature. HCl is eliminated and a double bond (C=C) forms:

$$CH_3-CH_2Cl + KOH \xrightarrow{\text{alc. KOH, }\Delta} CH_2=CH_2 + KCl + H_2O$$

*Ethyl chloride + Alc. KOH → Ethene + KCl + Water*

This is a **β-elimination reaction** (E2 mechanism). H is removed from the β-carbon (C adjacent to C bearing Cl), and Cl is removed, forming the double bond.

**Note:** Aqueous KOH would give **substitution** (ethanol), but **alcoholic** KOH gives **elimination** (ethene).

---

### 2) 1,2-Dibromoethane (CH₂Br–CH₂Br) → Ethyne (HC≡CH):

**Method: Double dehydrohalogenation**

1,2-Dibromoethane undergoes **two successive eliminations of HBr** using **alcoholic KOH**:

**Step 1:** Elimination of first HBr:
$$CH_2Br-CH_2Br + KOH \xrightarrow{\text{alc. KOH}} CHBr=CH_2 + KBr + H_2O$$
*(Vinyl bromide / bromoethene)*

**Step 2:** Elimination of second HBr using **sodamide (NaNH₂)** or excess alcoholic KOH:
$$CHBr=CH_2 + NaNH_2 \xrightarrow{\Delta} HC \equiv CH + NaBr + NH_3$$

**Overall:**
$$CH_2Br-CH_2Br \xrightarrow{2KOH \text{(alc.)}} HC \equiv CH + 2KBr + 2H_2O$$

*1,2-Dibromoethane → Ethyne (Acetylene)*

**6) a) Write any two postulates of V.B.T.**
**b) Write any two limitations of V.B.T.**

---

### a) Two Postulates of Valence Bond Theory (VBT):

**1. Orbital Overlap:**
A covalent bond is formed by the **overlapping of atomic orbitals** of two bonding atoms, each containing one electron with opposite spin. The greater the overlap, the stronger the bond formed.

$$A \cdot + \cdot B \rightarrow A:B \text{ (bond formed by overlap)}$$

**2. Types of bond based on overlap:**
- A **sigma (σ) bond** is formed by **axial (head-on) overlap** of orbitals along the internuclear axis. It is the first and strongest bond.
- A **pi (π) bond** is formed by **lateral (sideways) overlap** of parallel p-orbitals perpendicular to the internuclear axis. It is always formed in addition to a σ bond.

*(Other postulates include: paired electrons with opposite spins in bond; maximum overlap gives maximum bond strength; hybridization occurs before bonding to form equivalent orbitals.)*

---

### b) Two Limitations of Valence Bond Theory (VBT):

**1. Cannot explain paramagnetism of O₂:**
According to VBT, oxygen (O₂) should have all paired electrons (double bond, O=O). However, experimentally O₂ is found to be **paramagnetic** (it is attracted to a magnetic field), indicating it has **2 unpaired electrons**. VBT cannot explain this, but Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) can.

**2. Cannot explain the three-dimensional structure and bond angles correctly in some molecules:**
VBT with simple orbital overlap cannot satisfactorily explain the geometry and bond angles of many molecules. The concept of **hybridization** had to be artificially introduced to explain molecular shapes, which is not a natural part of the original VBT framework.

*(Other limitations: Does not explain delocalisation/resonance properly; cannot explain electron-deficient compounds; cannot explain colour of transition metal complexes.)*

**7) a) Define: (i) Aliphatic compound (ii) Heterocyclic Compound (iii) Aromatic Compound**
**b) Write any two uses of alkyne.**

---

### a) Definitions:

**(i) Aliphatic Compound:**
**Aliphatic compounds** are organic compounds that have **open-chain (acyclic) structures** — either straight chains or branched chains — or closed non-aromatic rings. They can be saturated (alkanes) or unsaturated (alkenes, alkynes).

The name comes from the Greek word *aleiphar* (fat), as fatty acids are aliphatic.

Examples: Methane (CH₄), Ethane (C₂H₆), Ethene (C₂H₄), Propanol (C₃H₇OH), Cyclohexane.

**(ii) Heterocyclic Compound:**
**Heterocyclic compounds** are cyclic (ring-containing) organic compounds in which the ring contains **at least one atom other than carbon** — such as nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), or sulphur (S) — as part of the ring. These non-carbon ring atoms are called **heteroatoms**.

Examples:
- Pyridine (ring has N)
- Furan (ring has O)
- Thiophene (ring has S)
- Pyrrole (ring has N)
- Glucose (open chain, but its ring form is heterocyclic with O)

**(iii) Aromatic Compound:**
**Aromatic compounds** are organic compounds that contain a **benzene ring** (or fused benzene rings) and satisfy **Hückel's rule** (contain 4n+2 π electrons, where n = 0, 1, 2...). They are characterized by **extra stability due to resonance/delocalization** of π electrons.

They preferentially undergo **electrophilic aromatic substitution** rather than addition reactions.

Examples: Benzene (C₆H₆), Toluene (C₆H₅CH₃), Naphthalene (C₁₀H₈), Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂).

---

### b) Two Uses of Alkynes:

1. **Acetylene (Ethyne) as a fuel — Oxy-acetylene welding and cutting:**
Acetylene (C₂H₂) burns in oxygen to produce an **oxy-acetylene flame** at approximately **3500°C** — one of the hottest flames achievable. This is used extensively in **welding (joining metals)** and **cutting (metal cutting)** in industry and construction.
$$2C_2H_2 + 5O_2 \rightarrow 4CO_2 + 2H_2O + \text{heat (3500°C)}$$

2. **Manufacture of important chemicals:**
Acetylene is a key raw material (starting material) in the manufacture of:
   - **Acetaldehyde** (by hydration of acetylene with H₂SO₄/HgSO₄)
   - **Acetic acid** (vinegar) — via acetaldehyde
   - **Synthetic rubber** (neoprene, polyvinyl chloride PVC — from vinyl chloride made from acetylene)
   - **Benzene** (by trimerisation of acetylene)
   - **Ethanol** (industrial alcohol)
